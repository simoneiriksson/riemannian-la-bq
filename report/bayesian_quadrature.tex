\section{Bayesian Quadrature}
A major issue with the Riemannian Laplace approximation is that it is very computationally expensive to solve the IVP-problem. In the following, we will discuss how to remedy this by using Bayesian Quadrature to find the integral in equation \ref{eq:posterior_predictive} in an efficient way. 

Bayesian Quadrature is a numerical method for integrating a function $f(\theta)$ over some measure $\pi(\theta)$: $J = \int f(\theta)\pi(\theta)d\theta$.
Briefly speaking, the assumption is that the function we want to integrate is drawn from a Gaussian Process distribution. If this is the case, the integral $J$ is a random variable and given a set of observations of $\theta$ and $f(\theta)$, the mean and variance of $J$ can be calculated. If the integration measure $\pi$ is Gaussian, the mean and variance can be found analytically \cite{Bayesian_quadrature}. The amount of variance reduction from a new datapoint is measured by the acquisition function, and by maximizing this function, it is computationally feasible to find the next value of $\theta$ that will minimize the variance of $J$. Figure \ref{fig:UFA_BQ_integrand_vals_1d} shows the Gaussian Process evaluated in three points, with its mean and variance. The corresponding function values and variance multiplied by the integration measure is seen in figure \ref{fig:UFA_BQ_integrand_vals_measure_1d}, and it can be seen here, that the optimal next point to evaluate must be between -1 and 0, since it is in that interval that we find the largest variance.

In our case, $f(\theta)=p(t^\ast\vert x^\ast, \theta)$ and $\pi(\theta) = p(\theta\vert \mathcal{D})$. 
As discussed in the previous section we approximate $p(\theta\vert \mathcal{D})$ by the Riemannian Laplace approximation which is denoted $q_\theta(\theta)$. If $v=\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)$ and the distribution of $v$ is given as $q_v(v)$, following \cite[p.29]{tomczak2024deep}  we get that

\begin{align}
q_\theta(\theta) &= q_v(\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)) \cdot \left\vert \det\left( \left. \frac{\partial \text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(z)}{\partial z}\right\vert_{z=\theta}\right) \right\vert \\
&= q_v(\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)) \cdot \left\vert \det\left( \left. \frac{\partial \text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(z)}{\partial z}\right\vert_{z=\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)}\right) \right\vert^{-1} \\
&= q_v(\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)) \cdot \left\vert J[{\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}}](\text{Log}_{\theta^\ast}(\theta)) \right\vert^{-1} \\
\end{align}
from which we can conclude that 
\begin{align*}
    q_v(v) = q_\theta(\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(v)) \cdot \left\vert J[{\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}}](v) \right\vert
\end{align*}
We can expand on the primary integral with integration by substitution and get
\begin{align*}
J 
&= \int f(\theta) q(\theta)d\theta \\
&= \int f(\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(v)) \cdot q_\theta(\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(v)) \cdot \vert J[{\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}}](v) \vert dv \\
&= \int f(\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(v)) \cdot q_v(v) \vert dv
\end{align*}
Since $q_v(v)$ is Gaussian, we can use this as measure for the Bayesian Quadrature, and then integrate the function $f(\text{Exp}_{\theta^\ast}(v))$.

In figure \ref{fig:UFA_BQ_variance}, the Bayesian Quadrature Riemannian Laplace approximation for the function approximator is plotted with the corresponding 95\% band for the mean and for the observations.
